The problem

Five tools, five logins, and no single answer to who can do what

We keep building small services because they work — an item code generator, an analytics hub, a template engine. Each arrives with its own address, its own login or none at all, and its own idea of who is allowed in. Access lives in a spreadsheet, in someone's memory, and in a shared PIN. There is no place an employee can see what they have, and no place IT can go to change it.

MM OS fixes the layer underneath, not the tools. Every service keeps running as it does now, in its own container with its own database. What changes is that they all learn who you are from one source.

The constraint that shapes everything

ERPNext is hosted software, so MM OS cannot own the traffic

ERPNext runs on Frappe Cloud. We do not control that server and cannot put our own middleware in front of it. So MM OS is not a gateway everything flows through — it is an identity, directory and workflow layer. It tells services who the visitor is; the services keep serving their own traffic directly.

That is also what makes it durable. Replace ERPNext or the CRM in two years and MM OS does not move.

Decisions

What we chose, and what each choice costs

Support and requests A separate Service Desk application, wearing the MM OS shell It serves every system including ERP and CRM, so it cannot live inside any one of them One more service to run

No separate identity server. A dedicated product like Keycloak earns its place once two or more third-party applications demand it. Because our tokens use the standard format, adding one later is configuration rather than a rebuild.

Three things people will notice

Getting in, getting back, and asking for what does not exist

One page to get in

Logo, sign in with Google or an employee code and PIN, and every service listed underneath. Click one and it opens — in-house tools sign you in through MM OS on the way, ERPNext and Twenty use their own sign-in on the same Google account.

Always one click back

A slim bar sits at the top of every service: back to MM OS, a searchable switcher showing only what you can open, your open request count, and your role right here. ERPNext gets the same back-link through its own navbar settings — a supported setting, not a workaround.

Requests that end in something built

Anyone can request an automation. IT replies with a real proposal — scope, days, server and AI resources, and the cheaper alternative if one exists. The department head approves the cost. Then it gets built and appears in the list.

The alternative field is mandatory in every proposal. It is the cheapest way to stop a department commissioning a service that a saved report would have solved, and it turns the manager's approval into a real choice rather than a signature.

The approver is worked out from the reporting line, never chosen by the requester, and what was approved is frozen so later growth in scope is visible. Everyone in a department can see the requests it raised and who is handling them; a request can be marked private, which keeps its detail between the requester, whoever is handling it and the approver.

Version one

What ships, and what deliberately waits

In v1

  • Google sign-in, plus code and PIN for shop-floor staff
  • All 74 employees, imported from the existing sheet
  • One entry page listing every service, filtered to your access once you are in
  • Access administration: who holds what, what each role permits, expiry dates, full history, and access requests decided in place
  • The shared bar and switcher across every service
  • ATT Platform and Item Code Studio moved across, their old PIN gates deleted
  • Service Desk: my requests, the department queue, the IT console and approvals
  • AI usage view with a per-service off switch
  • A complete record of who granted what, to whom, when

Waiting for v2

  • Unified mail inbox inside MM OS
  • Search across every service at once
  • Response-time targets and escalation on requests
  • Raising requests by email
  • AI cost dashboards and budgets
  • Drift report against ERPNext employee records

The inbox and email intake wait together on purpose, so they are designed once. The service registry built in v1 is what makes cross-service search possible later.

How it gets built

Nine agents, two runs

Every contract — the database, every endpoint, the token format, the integration rules — is written before any feature code. That is what lets six agents build at the same time without waiting on each other: none has to guess what another will produce, and no two ever open the same file.

Honest risks

What we are carrying on purpose

RiskHow it is handled
Removing access takes up to 60 secondsAccepted. Deactivating someone also ends their sessions immediately and stops new tokens at once, and disabling their Google account closes sign-in entirely.
Item Code Studio serves the public and admins from one applicationThree structural locks rather than discipline: every write sits behind one guard; the public list is an allowlist, so a forgotten page fails closed; and the public side reads through a database account with no write permission at all.
Service names are visible before signing inAccepted deliberately, so people can go straight to a tool they already use. Nothing is on the public internet, and that page carries names and addresses only — no roles, no people, no status.
Employee records exist in both MM OS and ERPNextMM OS is authoritative for access. A drift report is scheduled for v2 rather than pretended away.
AI usage depends on services reporting itA service that does not report shows as UNREPORTED — visibly, not as a blank row.
A private network is friction, and people route around frictionThe real adoption risk. Watched for, and answered with usability rather than policy memos.
VPN access lists rotMonthly audit of profiles against active employees, as a scheduled task with a command to run — not someone's memory.

Backups are only real once restored. A restore into a scratch database is part of the release criteria and repeats quarterly. An untested backup is a rumour.
